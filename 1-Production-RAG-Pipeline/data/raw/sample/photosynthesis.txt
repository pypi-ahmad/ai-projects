Photosynthesis is the process plants, algae, and some bacteria use to convert light energy into
chemical energy. Inside a plant cell, a green pigment called chlorophyll absorbs sunlight, mostly
in the chloroplasts of leaf cells. Using that captured energy, the plant combines carbon dioxide
taken in from the air with water drawn up through its roots. The reaction produces glucose, a
sugar the plant uses for growth and energy storage, and releases oxygen as a byproduct through
small pores in the leaves called stomata.

The overall reaction is often summarized as: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules
of water, in the presence of light, yield one molecule of glucose plus six molecules of oxygen.
Photosynthesis happens in two stages. The light-dependent reactions occur first and capture solar
energy to produce energy-carrying molecules. The light-independent reactions, sometimes called
the Calvin cycle, then use those energy carriers to build glucose from carbon dioxide, and this
second stage does not require light directly.

Photosynthesis is the foundation of most food chains on Earth, since it is how plants build the
organic matter that herbivores eat and that ultimately supports nearly all other life. It is also
the primary source of atmospheric oxygen, which most animals, including humans, depend on for
respiration.
