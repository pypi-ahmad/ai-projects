The water cycle, also called the hydrologic cycle, describes how water continuously moves
between the oceans, atmosphere, and land. The cycle has no true starting point, but it is often
described beginning with evaporation: heat from the Sun causes liquid water at the surface of
oceans, lakes, and rivers to turn into water vapor and rise into the atmosphere. Plants also
release water vapor into the air through a related process called transpiration.

As water vapor rises and cools at higher altitudes, it condenses around tiny particles of dust
or salt in the air to form clouds. When enough water condenses, it falls back to the surface as
precipitation, in the form of rain, snow, sleet, or hail depending on the temperature. Some of
this precipitation collects in rivers and lakes and eventually flows back to the ocean through a
process called runoff. Some of it soaks into the ground and becomes groundwater, which can
resurface later through springs or be taken up by plant roots.

The water cycle is powered almost entirely by energy from the Sun and is essential to nearly
every ecosystem on Earth, since it redistributes fresh water from the oceans to the land, where
it becomes available for drinking, agriculture, and countless other biological processes.
