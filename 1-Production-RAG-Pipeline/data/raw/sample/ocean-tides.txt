Ocean tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the gravitational pull of
the Moon, with a smaller contribution from the Sun. The Moon's gravity pulls the ocean water on
the side of Earth facing it into a bulge. At the same time, a second bulge forms on the opposite
side of the planet, largely because Earth itself is being pulled toward the Moon slightly more
than the ocean water on the far side is. As Earth rotates through these two bulges roughly once a
day, most coastlines experience two high tides and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes,
a cycle known as a semi-diurnal tide.

When the Sun, Moon, and Earth roughly line up, during a new moon or full moon, the Sun's and
Moon's gravitational effects reinforce each other and produce unusually large tidal ranges called
spring tides. When the Sun and Moon are roughly at right angles relative to Earth, during the
first or last quarter moon, their pulls partially cancel out and produce smaller tidal ranges
called neap tides.

Local geography also strongly affects how large a tide actually is at a given coastline. Narrow
bays and estuaries can amplify the tidal range considerably, while some enclosed seas experience
almost no noticeable tide at all. The Bay of Fundy in Canada is often cited as having some of the
largest tidal ranges in the world because of its funnel-shaped coastline.
